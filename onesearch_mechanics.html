OneSearch Starter Guide

Everything a first-time user needs: what OneSearch is, what to have ready before you start, how the process actually flows, and exactly what to say to Claude at each stage.

What this is

The Masterlist is one spreadsheet, one row per keyword, showing how that keyword performs across both organic search and paid search — for a given brand, over two time periods you're comparing (like this quarter vs. last quarter). It's built by Claude pulling together data from several sources and combining it into one list. You ask for it; Claude does the assembly.

Why this matters

Most reporting looks at organic and paid search separately — one team watches rankings, another watches ad spend, and nobody sees the full picture for a single keyword at once. The Masterlist exists to answer one question you can't easily answer from either side alone: for everything people search for, how much of that demand are we actually capturing — and are we capturing it for free, or paying for it?

That combined view is what surfaces the insights that matter most:

Wasted spend — you're paying for ads on a keyword you already rank #1 for organically. The Masterlist is where that becomes visible, because it's the only place clicks from both channels sit side by side.
Untapped demand — a keyword with huge search volume but almost no clicks from either channel. That's a content or bidding gap worth acting on.
What's actually driving results — which specific keywords and categories (competitor names, product types, recipes, etc.) are pulling their weight, and which aren't, once you can see both channels together.

Key terms, in plain language

Coverage

What share of a keyword's total search volume you're actually capturing in clicks — organic and paid combined. Low coverage on a high-volume keyword is usually the single most useful number on the whole sheet.

SEO vs. SEM

SEO = organic, unpaid search results (from Search Console). SEM = paid search ads (from Google Ads). Every performance column comes in both flavors so you can compare them directly.

Territory / Category

The bucket a keyword gets sorted into — Brand, Competitor, Recipe, and so on. This is what turns a flat list of thousands of keywords into groups you can actually make decisions about.

Purchase intent

A label (from SE Ranking) describing what kind of search this is — commercial (ready to buy), informational (researching), navigational (looking for a specific site), or local.

Defining territories for a brand

Territories are what turn a raw list of thousands of keywords into a dashboard you can actually read — but there's no automated wizard for setting them up yet. It's a short, one-time step someone does by hand (with Claude's help) before a brand's first dashboard build.

1

Look at what the data actually contains

Before inventing categories, look at a sample of real keywords for this brand — what actually shows up matters more than what you'd guess in the abstract.

2

Pick the territories that make sense for this brand's category

Not every brand needs the same buckets. A yogurt brand's territories (Brand, Competitor, Source of Protein, Recipe...) don't fit a coffee-creamer or plant-milk brand — those need their own set, built around their own products and competitors.

3

Write each one up

For each territory: a short name, a one-or-two sentence description of what belongs in it, a few real example keywords, and a coverage target — the "good coverage" benchmark for that category (branded terms usually target much higher coverage than broad generic ones).

4

These live in the brand's config, not the sheet

Territory definitions are written into that brand's setup file, not into the Masterlist itself — ask Claude to add them once you've decided what they should be.

i

This step is optional, not required. Skip it and the dashboard still groups keywords automatically using whatever categories are already in the data — it just won't have friendly descriptions, a deliberate display order, or coverage benchmarks until someone defines them. Worth doing before a dashboard goes in front of a client; not a blocker for an internal first look.

The exports, in detail

What each one actually is, where it comes from, and what it's for.

Search Console

Required
Where it comes from
Google Search Console → Performance report → Queries, exported for both comparison periods.
What's in it
For every search query: organic clicks, impressions, click-through rate, and average ranking position.
What it's for
The "did we show up and get clicked, for free" half of the picture.

Google Ads Search Terms

Required
Where it comes from
Google Ads → Reports → Search terms report, exported for both comparison periods.
What's in it
The actual search terms that triggered a paid ad — clicks, cost, impressions, and which campaign/keyword matched.
What it's for
The "did we pay to show up, and how much" half of the picture.

SE Ranking

Required
Where it comes from
SE Ranking (third-party rank tracker) — a keyword export for the brand's tracked set, most recent month.
What's in it
Current ranking position, estimated monthly search volume, CPC, and Google's own search-intent label, per keyword.
What it's for
Market-level numbers — total demand and typical cost — that Search Console and Ads don't report on their own.

Keyword Study

Required
Where it comes from
The brand's own internal keyword taxonomy sheet, maintained by the SEO/strategy team.
What's in it
Every relevant keyword for the brand's category, tagged with topic, category, sub-category, and monthly search volume history.
What it's for
Turns a flat list of thousands of keywords into meaningful groups — Brand, Competitor, Recipe, and so on.

GA4 Conversions

Optional
Where it comes from
Google Analytics 4, filtered to the brand's specific conversion events (e.g. "click to buy," store-locator clicks), exported per landing page.
What's in it
How many of those conversion events happened on each page.
What it's for
Connects organic traffic to actual outcomes, not just clicks. Missing it just leaves conversion numbers blank.

GA4 Ads Sessions

Optional
Where it comes from
Google Analytics 4, filtered to sessions that came from Google Ads specifically — session count and conversions per landing page.
What's in it
Enough detail to calculate a real paid-conversion number per keyword, instead of a rough estimate.
What it's for
Two specific columns depend on this alone. Missing it leaves just those two waiting — nothing else is affected.

How the sources actually combine

This is the part that's easy to get wrong intuitively: the six sources don't all merge the same way. Two of them merge directly; two get matched on afterward; and the conversion data attaches through a completely different path — the page it happened on, not the keyword itself.

When the Keyword Study is missing volume

Sometimes a keyword doesn't match anything in the Keyword Study, or the Keyword Study simply doesn't have volume data for the current time period — this happened for both new brands onboarded so far. Claude doesn't leave those numbers blank if it can help it.

1

After the Masterlist is written, it's checked again

Every keyword that's actually active — it has real clicks, or a partial volume number — but is still missing search volume gets flagged.

2

Those keywords get looked up directly

Claude queries the SE Ranking tool's own database by keyword text — a second, independent path to volume data, separate from whatever the Keyword Study had.

3

The result fills in both places

The overall average search volume, and the individual monthly numbers, for whichever keywords needed it.

i

This only recovers volume for keywords that are already clearly active — it's not trying to guess a number for every blank row. It also requires an SE Ranking API key to be configured; ask Claude to confirm one is set up if a brand's volume numbers seem sparser than expected.

Reviewing uncertain matches

Every Masterlist has a companion review tab in the same Google Sheet for keywords Claude wasn't confident enough to match automatically. Here's what to actually do with it:

1

Open the review tab

It sits in the same spreadsheet as the Masterlist, usually named something like "KW Review."

2

Look at the suggested match

Each row shows the keyword, Claude's best guess at what it matches, and a similarity score — the closer to 1, the more confident the guess.

3

Approve, correct, or skip

If the suggestion looks right, mark it approved. If it's close but wrong, you can write in the correct match yourself. If neither, just leave it — it stays out of the Masterlist rather than getting a wrong answer.

4

Ask Claude to pick up your approvals

Once you've gone through the tab, say so — Claude re-reads it and writes the approved rows into the Masterlist.

This step is optional and can happen any time after the first build — it's not a blocker to using the rest of the Masterlist in the meantime.

Correcting taxonomy & competitor misclassification

The Keyword Study match is the primary source of a row's category, but it's never exhaustive — a competitor's product line that isn't in the Keyword Study yet, or wasn't matched confidently enough, falls through to a generic bucket. "flavors of natural bliss coffee creamer" reading as a generic product query instead of a Natural Bliss competitor query is exactly this kind of gap. There's a dedicated, config-driven fix for it.

The competitors block in a brand's config

Every brand's config.json can carry a competitors list — each entry is a real competitor name plus the literal text patterns that identify it in a keyword:

{ // brands/international-delight/config.json "competitors": [ { "name": "Coffee Mate", "patterns": ["coffee mate", "coffeemate", "nestle"] }, { "name": "Natural Bliss", "patterns": ["natural bliss"] }, { "name": "Starbucks", "patterns": ["starbucks"] } ] }

Every pipeline run checks every keyword against every brand's competitor patterns automatically — this needs no per-run request, it's a native step now. When a keyword contains one of these patterns, that overrides whatever category the row already had, including a real Keyword Study match — naming a competitor's product makes it a competitor query first, regardless of what else it superficially resembles.

i

When two competitors match the same keyword (e.g. "coffee mate natural bliss creamer" names both Coffee Mate and its own Natural Bliss sub-line), the longest matching pattern wins — not whichever competitor happens to be listed first in the config. That's what correctly puts Natural Bliss queries under Natural Bliss even when the parent brand's name is also present. This isn't a detail you need to manage day to day, but it's worth knowing if a competitor tag ever looks wrong on a keyword that names two brands at once.

Where the candidate list for a new brand comes from

You don't have to invent a brand's competitor list from scratch. Two starting points, in order of how ready-to-use they are:

1

The registry's own "COMPETITORS" row

The same source-config sheet that holds each brand's export links (Doc IDs, tabs) can also carry one row per brand labeled COMPETITORS, with a pipe-delimited list of candidate terms in the URL column. Every pipeline run reads this row automatically and prints any term not yet reflected in that brand's config.json — a nudge, not an auto-apply. It never writes classification from this row directly.

2

Carla Klaasen's Keyword Study filter formulas

The older shared Danone multiverse document has a "Topical cluster" tab with a FILTER/REGEXMATCH formula per brand, built for a different purpose (filtering out irrelevant queries from a Keyword Study), but its first regex clause lists every brand/competitor name the analyst had identified at the time. It's a genuinely useful reference — but it was never built to be a clean competitor list on its own.

⚑

Do not copy either list into config.json as-is. Both mix real competitors with noise — retailers (Kroger, Walmart, Amazon, Whole Foods), unrelated brands, and generic terms that happen to co-occur in the same filtered-out query set. When Silk's registry row was reviewed this way, terms like kroger, walmart, trader joe, vitamix, can, and powder were deliberately left out. A term also needs a judgment call if it names something real but not a true external competitor — International Delight's row listed dunkin, which is Dunkin' Creamers, a fellow Danone-owned brand, not a market competitor; and splanda, almost certainly a typo for Splenda, a sweetener ingredient brand rather than a competing creamer. Both were flagged in that brand's config comment instead of added. Treat every candidate list as a first pass to review line by line, never a paste target.

What this mechanism can't do

Competitor classification only fires on keywords that literally name a competitor in their text. It has no visibility into which brands actually show up in the search results for a generic query — "zero sugar coffee creamer" names no competitor, even on a day when the top organic results are wall-to-wall Silk, Nutpods, and MALK instead of the usual Coffee Mate/Chobani mix that dominates flavor and seasonal searches. That's a real, useful pattern to notice — it just lives in SERP composition data, not keyword text, so it has to be observed by checking rankings directly rather than read off the Masterlist's CATEGORY column. If this turns out to matter enough to track over time, it would need its own data source and its own dashboard cut — flag it for a scoping conversation rather than expecting the existing classifier to surface it.

What this is

The dashboard is the shareable, client-facing report — an interactive page built from a finished Masterlist. It never looks at the raw exports directly, so it's only ever as good as the Masterlist behind it.

What's actually on it

One file, four things to know how to read:

The keyword table

Every row from the Masterlist, sortable and filterable — this is the detail view for anyone who wants to dig into a specific keyword or category.

Coverage stats

The headline numbers — how much of total search demand is being captured, overall and broken out by category, for both time periods so you can see the trend.

Territory Deep Dive

Performance grouped by category (Brand, Competitor, Recipe, etc.) — this is usually where the real story is, since a single overall number hides which categories are actually driving results.

Recommendations

Auto-generated notes on what to prioritize next, based on the coverage and spend patterns in the data — a starting point for discussion, not a final verdict.

What you need before you start

A Masterlist that's already been through a full build for this brand — not a small test run
A sense of who's going to see this — internal review, or a client? That changes how much you should double-check first

How the Recommendations panel actually works

The write-up behind the Recommendations panel lives in its own small file per brand, separate from the Masterlist — this is the one part of the dashboard that's genuinely written, not just computed from the data.

What the file actually contains

One file per brand (content.json), with exactly two things in it:

{ // one card per recommendation — this is what renders in the panel "recommendations": [ { "id": "01", "channel": "SEO", "priority": "High", "quarter": "Q2", "category": "PRODUCT", "title": "Expand product page coverage for high-volume keywords", "bullets": [ "Create pages for the top uncovered high-volume queries", "Target keywords with >10K monthly searches and <3% coverage" ] } ], // short narrative blurbs, one per dashboard section — optional, often left blank "commentary": { "intro": "", "territory_deepdive": "", "recommendations": "", "quality_score": "" } }

Recommendations are the actual cards a viewer sees — each one needs a title, a priority, a category, and a few bullet points, and Claude can draft all of this once a Masterlist exists. Commentary is a separate, smaller thing: short freeform narrative text for specific sections of the dashboard (the intro, the Territory Deep Dive, etc.) — it's fine to leave any of these blank, the dashboard just shows that section without extra commentary.

1

Ask Claude to draft it

Once a Masterlist exists, ask Claude to draft recommendations based on what it's actually showing — coverage gaps, high-volume/low-coverage keywords, where competitor spend is winning. Each recommendation becomes a short card: a title, a priority, a category, and a few bullet points.

2

Review and edit the wording

Read it like you'd read a first draft from a colleague — adjust tone, cut anything generic, make sure it actually reflects the brand's situation. This is the one place worth reading closely before it goes anywhere.

3

Rebuild to see it live

While you're still iterating, the dashboard reads this file fresh each time you open it — so the recommendations file needs to stay in the same folder as the dashboard while you're working on it.

4

Finalize when you're happy with it

Ask Claude to finalize the dashboard once the wording is settled. This bakes the recommendations permanently into the HTML file itself — the dashboard no longer needs the separate file to display them correctly.

i

"Removing the JSON file" in practice: once finalized, the standalone dashboard HTML is the complete deliverable — you can share just that one file, and the separate recommendations file doesn't need to travel with it. Keep the original around anyway if you might want to update the wording later; editing the finalized HTML directly doesn't survive a rebuild.
